\section{Experiments and Results}
\subsection{Simulation Experiments and Results Analysis}
To rigorously validate the proposed Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-driven Joint Communication and Control (JCC) framework,
comprehensive cyber-physical simulations were conducted. This chapter details the experimental setup, analyzes the training 
dynamics of the DRL agent, and presents a deep comparative evaluation against traditional allocation heuristic under diverse 
extreme bandwidth constraints.
\subsubsection{Simulation Engine and Manipulator Configuration}
The experiments were deployed utilizing the \textbf{PyBullet} physics engine, which provides high-fidelity, rigid-body kinematic and dynamic simulations at 240 Hz. 
The physical plant under control is the \textbf{Franka Emika Panda}, an industry-standard 7-DOF robotic manipulator. 
The robot is initialized near a nomial safe home posture ($q_{home} = [0, -\pi/4, 0, -3\pi/4, 0, \pi/2, \pi/4]^T$) to prevent 
initial kinematic singularities.

\subsubsection{Dual-Frequency Decoupling and DPCM Communication Channel}
A critical innovation in our experimental design is the realistic modeling of the network channel. Traditional simulations often 
erroneously equate the physical control frequency with the communication frequency, leading to artificial high-frequency quantization 
chattering (the "Parkinson's effect" in motors). 

To accurately reflect real-world Networked Control Systems (NCS), we implemented a \textbf{Dual-Frequency Decoupling Architecture}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Control Frequency ($f_{ctrl}$)}: The physical engine and local Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) motor drives operate at \textbf{240 Hz}, ensuring smooth torque interpolation.
    \item \textbf{Communication Frequency ($f_{comm}$)}:  The edge network node transmits quantized Differential Pulse Code Modulation (DPCM) payloads at 10 Hz.
\end{enumerate}
Within each 0.1-second communication interval, the maximum physical angular displacement is constrained to $\Delta_{max} = 0.2$ rad, 
aligning with the hardware velocity limits of the Franka actuators.
\subsubsection{Evaluation Tasks and Baselines}
The framework was evaluated on standardized 3D pick-and-place tasks comprising three distinct, randomized trajectories to prevent policy overfitting. 
Each trajectory consists of reaching a \textbf{Hover Point}, descending to a \textbf{Grasp Point}, and transferring to a \textbf{Placement Point}.

The proposed DRL dynamic allocator is benchmarked against two theoretical baselines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Allocation (Average)}: Evenly distributes bits across all 7 joints.
    \item \textbf{Static LQR Allocation}: A state-of-the-art mathematical baseline utilizing a static Jacobian sensitivity matrix computed at $q_{home}$ to perform logarithmic water-filling allocation.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Training Dynamics and Competitive Convergence}
Training an intelligent agent to optimize bandwidth allocation is highly susceptible to the "Lazy Agent Trap"—where the agent stops exploring once it marginally beats a single baseline. 
To enforce absolute global optimality, the DRL agent was trained using a \textbf{Competitive Multi-Baseline Reward} for 300,000 steps.

At each episode, the spatial tracking error ($E_{RL}$) is simultaneously compared against the Uniform error ($E_{avg}$) and the 
Static LQR error ($E_{static}$). The reward is shaped as a blended competitive advantage:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \text{clip}\Big( (E_{static} - E_{RL}) + 0.5(E_{avg} - E_{RL}), -50, 50 \Big)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The Collapse of Static Models at Extreme Bottlenecks (14-bit)}
The most striking academic discovery occurs at the 14-bit limit (averaging 2bits/joint). Here, the mathematically derived \textbf{Static LQR suffers a catastrophic collapse},
yielding a massive Grasp Error of 48.85mm, significantly underperforming the naive Uniform allocation (27.67 mm).

This collapse physically proves the fundamental limitation of time-invariant models: \textbf{Jacobian Shifting}. 
The Static LQR weights are computed at the initial safe posture. 
When the manipulator stretches outward, its nonlinear kinematics fundamentally alter the sensitivity matrix. 
The static algorithm rigidly forces bits to joints that are no longer critical, actively sabotaging the end-effector's precision.

In stark contrast, the proposed \textbf{DRL agent achieves a commanding Grasp Error of 15.65 mm}. 
By utilizing its real-time 3D spatial observation, the neural network implicitly acts as a dynamic Jacobian estimator, gracefully degrading the precision of distal wrist joints to secure high-resolution transmission for the heavily loaded base joints.
\subsection{Ablation Studies and Engineering Considerations}
To isolate contributions of specifc architectural innovations within our framework, a series of ablation studies were conducted.
These studies highlight the critical engineering considerations required to bridge the gap between theoretical algorithms and realistic 
physical simulations.
\subsubsection{The Necessity of Steady-State Evaluation (Addressing Dynamic Tracking Lag)}
Initial evaluations of the system measured the continuous tracking error while the manipulator was in full motion. However, this resulted 
in an artificially inflated baseline error (e.g.,\textasciitilde 120mm) across all allocation schemes, regardless of the available bandwidth.

\textbf{Analysis}: In physical systems, high-inertia motors operating at a decoupled 10 Hz communication rate constantly chase "stale" waypoints,
creating a massive \textbf{Dynamic Tracking Lag}. If quantization accuracy is measured during this transient phase, the physical inertia completely 
masks the underlying bit-allocation performance. \textbf{Solution}: By enforcing a 0.5-second \textbf{Settle Phase} (steady-state) at critical waypoints 
before measuring the error, the physical momentum is nullified. This ablation mathematically isolates the pure quantization noise, proving 
that traditional continuous-trajectory metrics are fundamentally flawed for evaluating low-frequency Networked Control Systems.
\subsubsection{The "Seesaw Effect" and 3D Spatial Awareness Injection}
During early training iterations, the RL agent was provided a standard 28-dimensional state vector (comprising only joint angles and velocities). 
This led to the \textbf{"Seesaw Effect"}: the agent would achieve excellent precision at Grasp Point but fail catastrophically at the Placement Point.

\textbf{Analysis}: Joint angles are highly abstract. Without explicit Cartesian coordinates, the agent lacks "Spatial Awareness" and fails to semantically differentiate between distinct operational phases (e.g., reaching vs. grasping).
\textbf{Solution}: By expanding the observation space to \textbf{34 dimensions} (injecting the real-time $X,Y,Z$ coordinates of both the end-effector and the target), 
the agent gained a comprehensive global spatial awareness. This spatial injection eliminated the Seesaw Effect, allowing the policy to dynamically switch distinct bit-allocation 
strategies based on its absolute position in the workspace. 
\subsection{Scenario Two: Underactuated Quadrotor Navigation and Survival}
To prove that the proposed DRL allocator acts as a universal, 
paradigm-agnostic JCC framework, a supplementary cyber-physical evaluation was conducted using a Quadrotor UAV. 
Unlike the fully-actuated mechanical manipulator, which simply halts or deviates when bandwidth is insufficient, an underactuated quadrotor represents a "life-or-death" control scenario where severe quantization noise leads to immediate aerodynamic stalling and catastrophic crashing.
\subsubsection{Experimental Setup and Extreme Bottleneck}
The UAV simulation was constructed using the PyBullet physics engine. The total channel capacity was severely choked to a strict $B_{total} = 24$ bits per transmission to carry the 12-dimensional state vector (averaging a mere 2 bits per state). 
The communication frequency remained decoupled at 10 Hz, while the inner-loop PID flight controller operated at 240 Hz based on the ZOH-reconstructed quantized states.

To comprehensively test the robustness and adaptability of the allocation schemes, the evaluation was divided into two distinct, highly hostile operational scenarios:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scenario A - Severe Wind Hovering (Wind Level: 2.0)}: The UAV is commanded to maintain a stationary hover while being subjected to a continuous, severe wind disturbance. This tests the baseline survival capability.
    \item \textbf{Scenario B - Dynamic Maneuvering under Strong Wind (Wind Level: 1.5)}: The UAV maintains a hover for the first 5.0 seconds. At exactly $T = 5.0$ s, a sudden 2.5-meter waypoint displacement command is injected. This tests the allocator's ability to dynamically switch context between "attitude stabilization" and "positional navigation".
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{1. Scenario A: The Emergence of Pragmatic Prioritization}: Under the severe wind conditions of Scenario A, the naive Uniform allocation 
scheme collapse rapidly (5.2 seconds). Distributing an equal 2 bits across dimension failed to provide the necessary resolution for the critical ($\phi$) and pitch ($\theta$) axes.

Both the Static LQR and the proposed DRL agent successfully maintained stable flight for the full 20-second duration. 
The Static LQR survived because its predefined, mathematically derived sensitivity formula heavily favored the attitude axes, which perfectly matched the requirements for a stationary hover. 
However, the DRL agent achieved this exact expert-level prioritization organically. Without any predefined sensitivity matrices, the agent independently discovered the fundamental aerodynamic truth:
spatial coordinates $(X,Y)$ must be sacrificed to channel bandwidth towards attitude stabilization.

\noindent\textbf{2. Scenario B: Extended Survivability via Dynamic Borrowing}: The true superiority and context-awareness of the DRL framework emerged spectacularly in Scenario B. At $T = 5.0$ s, the sudden 2.5-meter maneuver command fundamentally altered the task dynamics.

The Static LQR, bound by its time-invariant formula, suffered a rapid, catastrophic collapse, crashing at $T=6.1$ s (merely 1.1 seconds after the command). 
Because the static matrix rigidly starved the $X$ and $Y$ positional axes of bits regardless of the situation, the flight controller was entirely blind to the new waypoint, leading to chaotic acceleration and terminal roll-over.

In sharp contrast, the \textbf{DRL agent demonstrated remarkable Extended Survivability}. Although the ultimate physical limitations 
of the 24-bit channel inevitably restrict infinite survival during such conflicting demands (high-speed maneuver + strong wind), the DRL agent successfully extended the operational survival window to $T = 8.6$ s, enduring over 3$\times$ longer than the Static LQR post-command.

This extended resilience is attributed to the DRL's \textbf{Context-Aware Dynamic Borrowing capability}. By continuously monitoring the 36-dimensional observation space (which includes real-time tracking errors), the DRL agent dynamically recognized the target displacement.
It proactively extracted a few bits from the attitude axes to momentarily provide critical positional updates to the $X$-axis, while attempting to balance the aerodynamic stability.
This intelligent, real-time arbitration proves that the proposed DRL framework transcends rigid mathematical matrices, capable of executing \textit{Graceful Degradation} to dynamically extend system survivability at the absolute limits of Shannon channel capacity.
