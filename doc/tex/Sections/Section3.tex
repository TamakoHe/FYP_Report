\section{Chapter 3:Cyber-Physical Experiments and Results}
This chapter presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of proposed Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-drivern Joint Communication and Control (JCC) 
framework. A fundamental objective of this study is to demonstrate that proposed intelligent bandwidth allocation strategy is not merely overfitted to a specific 
robotic configuration, but rather serves as universal, \textbf{cross-morphology generalization framework} capable of addressing extreme communication bottlenecks across diverse cyber-physical paradigms.

To rigorously validate this universality, the experimental methodology is structured around a progressive, tri-fold validation approach:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-DOF Fully-Actuated Spatial Allocation}: Utilizing a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda manipulator to demonstrate the framework's capability to extract maximum spatial precision and execute "graceful degradation" under severe quantization noise.
    \item \textbf{Underactuated High-Dynamic Survival}: Employing a 12-dimensional Quadrotor UAV simulation subjected to extreme wind disturbances to validate the agent's pragmatic "survival prioritization" capabilities in life-or-death control scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Sim-to-Real Edge Deployment}: Leveraging a customized 4-DOF physical manipulator and its exact Digital Twin to verify the algorithm's robustness against real-world static friction, unmodeled hardware latency, and asymmetric payload dynamics.
\end{itemize}
Through these progressive scenarios, the empirical results will highlight the fundamental limitations of traditional Shannon-separated uniform transmission and static mathematical allocations, 
ultimately proving the superiority of context-aware dynamic resource scheduling.
\subsection{Experimental Setup and Implementation Details}
To comprehensively evaluate the performance and robustness of the JCC architecture, a rigorous multi-stage validation methodology 
was established. This section outlines the unified configuration of the simulation environments, the physical plants under investigation,
and the standardized evaluation baselines, ensuring the complete reproducibility of the experiments presented in this chapter.
\subsubsection{Simulation Engine and DRL Pipeline}
The primary testbeds for training the reinforcement learning agents and validating the extreme bandwidth constraints were constructed using the PyBullet physics engine. 
PyBullet was selected due to its highly optimized rigid-body dynamics, accurate collision detection algorithms, and robust support for simulating diverse robotic kinematics. 
To accurately capture the non-linear physical dynamics and transient tracking errors without introducing artificial simulator stability, the physics engine consistently operates with a base high-frequency control step of 240 Hz.

Due to the substantial computational overhead required for real-time Forward Kinematics (FK), Jacobian matrix calculations, and dynamic collision detection during the continuous DRL training phase, a multiprocessing vectorized architecture was implemented.
Utilizing the \inlinecode{SubprocVecEnv} wrapper from the \inlinecode{stable-baselines3} library, the training environments were parallelized 
across independent, headless PyBullet physics servers on a multi-core CPU. This parallelized paradigm enables the agent to explore 
hundreds of thousands of complex physical interactions simultaneously, drastically accelerating convergence while preventing the policy from overfitting 
to a single state trajectory.

\subsubsection{Target Cyber-Physical Plants}
To scientifically prove that the proposed framework transcends specific hardware limitations, the evaluations were conducted across three fundamentally different physical plants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{7-DOF Franka Emika Panda Manipulator}: A high-dimensional, fully-actuated, and kinematically redundant system. This plant is utilized to validate the agent's capacity for extreme spatial precision extraction under severe bandwidth bottlenecks, forcing the algorithm to learn the complex mechanical leverage effect between base and wrist joints.
    \item \textbf{Quadrotor UAV (12-Dimensional State)}: A classic underactuated and highly unstable dynamic system. Unlike a robotic arm that merely halts upon communication failure, a UAV losing spatial feedback instantly crashes. It is employed to demonstrate the framework's "Survival Prioritization" capability—sacrificing spatial coordinates to guarantee the transmission of critical attitude (roll/pitch) data under extreme wind disturbances.
    \item \textbf{Custom 4-DOF Manipulator (Digital Twin \& Physical Hardware)}: A cost-effective, resource-constrained edge device. This customized plant serves as the ultimate Sim-to-Real validation testbed. A precise Digital Twin was constructed in PyBullet for pre-training, after which the policy was deployed to the physical hardware to test its robustness against real-world static friction and unmodeled payload dynamics.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Baseline Methodologies}
To benchmark the superiority of the DRL-driven dynamic allocator, two standardized spatial allocation baselines were defined and uniformly applied across all experimental scenarios:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Allocation (Average)}: A naive, mathematically straightforward baseline that evenly distributes the total available bandwidth ($B_{total}$) across all $n$ control dimensions. While computationally zero-cost, it completely ignores the diverse physical sensitivities of different states, treating a critical base joint exactly the same as a distal wrist joint.
    \item \textbf{Static LQR-Weighted Allocation}: A state-of-the-art mathematical baseline. It utilizes the infinite-horizon Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) cost function and the system's static Jacobian sensitivity matrix (computed at the nominal home posture) to perform logarithmic water-filling bit assignment. While this scheme successfully prioritizes sensitive joints initially, it suffers heavily from "Jacobian Shifting"—its fixed formulas tend to collapse when the highly non-linear system operates far from the pre-calculated home posture.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Simulation Scenario I: High-DOF Spatial Allocation (Franka Manipulator)}
To empirically validate the superiority of the proposed Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-driven Joint Communication and Control (JCC) framework, 
a series of comprehensive simulations were conducted utilizing the high-fidelity PyBullet physical engine. 
This section systematically evaluates the framework's performance under severe bandwidth constraints, benchmarking the intelligent PPO-based dynamic allocator against traditional uniform allocation and static LQR-weighted allocation strategies on a high-DOF manipulator.
\subsubsection{Training Convergence and Reward Analysis}
Before evaluating the final tracking performance of the robotic manipulator, it is imperative to analyze the training dynamics and convergence behavior of the PPO agent. 
Training a DRL agent within a highly non-linear, rigid-body physics environment presents significant challenges, particularly due to the discontinuous nature of extreme bit quantization and the resulting high-variance physical feedback.

As vividly captured in Figure.~\ref{fig:reward} the training convergence process exhibits a highly stable and monotonic ascent, which profoundly reflects the agent's complex learning behavior. This progression can be analyzed through three distinct phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Exploration and High Penalty Phase (Evaluations 0 to 5)}: In the initial stages of training, the Actor network's weights are randomly initialized, leading to stochastic bandwidth allocation across the 7-DOF joints. Because the agent has not yet learned the mechanical "lever effect" of the manipulator, its random policy performs significantly worse than the predefined Static LQR and Uniform baselines. Consequently, the agent receives a severe competitive penalty, initiating at roughly $-20$. This phase perfectly validates the necessity of the competitive reward shaping; the environment yields consistent, unyielding penalties, coercing the agent to desperately search for a viable gradient out of the sub-optimal allocation trap.
    \item \textbf{Rapid Ascent and Baseline Breakthrough Phase (Evaluations 5 to 15)}: Following the initial exploratory penalty, the reward curve exhibits a steep and continuous ascent. Driven by the 34-dimensional 3D spatial awareness injection, the PPO agent rapidly discovers the fundamental principle of \textit{Pragmatic Communication}: proactively stripping bits from robust, distal joints to unconditionally secure high-precision transmission for critical base joints. The most critical academic milestone occurs around \textbf{Evaluation 14}, where the reward curve successfully \textbf{crosses the zero-axis ($r>0$)}. In our reward formulation, crossing this threshold mathematically signifies that the DRL agent has officially surpassed the blended tracking performance of both the Static LQR and the Uniform average baselines, transitioning from a sub-optimal novice to a highly intelligent allocator.
    \item \textbf{Steady-State Convergence Phase (Evaluations 15 to 25)}: After decisively beating the theoretical baselines, the reward continuous to climb and gradually plateaus into a smooth steady-state, stabilizing at an asymptotic maximum of approximately $+2.5$. Notably, the curve exhibits no catastrophic forgetting or violent trust-region oscillations typical of complex continuous control tasks. This perfect, smooth convergence is heavily supported by the linear learning rate scheduling and the clipping mechanism implemented within into the PPO environment. This dynamic scheduling forces the agent to seamlessly transition from aggressive exploration to conservation exploitation, securely "locking in" the learned pragmatic allocation policy for extreme bandwidth scenarios.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Spatial Domain: Scalability and Performance under Diverse Constraints}
Following the verification of the DRL agent's stable convergence, this experiment investigates the core spatial domain performance of the proposed Joint Communication and Control (JCC) framework.
The primary objective is to evaluate how effectively the intelligent agent arbitrates limited network resources across the 7-DOF manipulator compared to traditional heuristics, and crucially, how this performance scales across different degrees of channel degradation.

To comprehensively assess scalability and ensure that the RL policy generalizes rather than overfitting to a single maneuver, the evaluations were conducted across \textbf{three distinct pick-and-place trajectories}. The total bandwidth constraint ($B_{total}$) per sampling interval was systematically swept from a saturated regime (56 bits) down to an extreme "survival-level" bottleneck (14 bits).

The accurately simulate a real-world networked control system (NCS), the communication frequency was decoupled from the high-frequency 
physical control loop. The control commands were transmitted at 10 Hz over the simulated JCC channel using Differential Pulse Code Modulation (DPCM), while the PyBullet physics engine executed the tracking interpolation at 240 Hz. Three spatial allocation schemes were benchmarked:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uniform Allocation (Average)}: A naive baseline that evenly distributes $B_{total}/7$ bits to each joint.
    \item \textbf{Static LQR-Weighted Allocation}: A mathematically derived baseline utilizing the static Jacobian sensitivity matrix (computed at the home posture) to perform logarithmic water-filling bit assignment.
    \item \textbf{DRL Dynamic Allocation (Proposed)}: The context-aware PPO agent that continuously redistributes bits based on the real-time 3D spatial observation ($s_t$).
\end{itemize}
